How we know what is new

Our team read and reviewed the dashboards, code and reports of all 29 other groups in the course, one review per group, and catalogued every method, check and feature they used, with which groups used it (154 items). Results

Degree and citation distributions

Fraction of nodes (or papers) with at least x links (or citations), log–log. The line is the power law fitted above xmin by maximum likelihood (Clauset, Shalizi & Newman 2009); a bootstrap p ≥ 0.1 means a power law is plausible. The table below tests it against other heavy-tailed shapes.

Small world?

Clustering C and average path length L of the largest component, against Erdős–Rényi graphs with the same nodes and links and against degree-preserving rewired graphs. Small world: L close to random, C far above it (σ = (C/Cr)/(L/Lr) ≫ 1).

Power law against the alternatives

Log-likelihood ratio R on the fitted tail (positive favours the power law) with Vuong's p-value; for the nested power law with cutoff, a χ² test. p ≥ 0.1 = the data cannot tell the two apart.

Removing faculty

Largest connected group (share of all 274 faculty) as faculty leave: at random (mean of 100 orders), highest degree first, highest betweenness first, or most cross-lab co-authors first (brokers), recomputed after each removal.

Do the four labs stay connected?

Lab pairs (of 6) still joined by some chain of co-authors as faculty are removed, same orders as on the left.

How fast an idea could spread

SI model: start from one faculty member; each step, every joint paper with an "infected" colleague passes it on with probability 0.05. Median steps until another lab, and every lab, is reached; one run from every faculty member in the connected group. Null: links rewired at random (same degrees), so lab walls disappear.

Epidemic threshold (SIS)

Stationary share infected in an SIS model (recovery 0.2 per step) against the spreading rate λ = β/μ, scaled by the quenched mean-field threshold 1/Λmax (largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix). Theory predicts the outbreak survives right of the dashed line.

Predicting next year's collaborations

For each test year, every pair of active faculty not yet linked is scored using only earlier years; AUC = chance that a pair that does link that year outranks one that does not (0.5 = guessing). The logistic model combines all scores and is trained on earlier years only.

What makes a new collaboration

Odds ratios from a discrete-time model of the first joint paper of each unlinked pair, 2017–2025, with year effects. Bars: 95% intervals with dyadic-robust errors (observations sharing a person may be correlated). log scale.

Tie-formation models

Coefficients as odds ratios with dyadic-robust 95% intervals. Shared co-authors = log(1 + common neighbours); degree = log(1 + degree product); output = log(1 + papers) summed; seniority from the CSIR grade on the roster (1 Scientist … 6 fellows and directors).

Influence or similarity? The shuffle test

Effect of having an adopting co-author (odds ratio, log) in the real data against 200 data sets where adoption years are shuffled among the same adopters (Anagnostopoulos et al. 2008). If co-authors influenced each other, the real effect would sit to the right of the shuffled ones; if it sits inside them, the pattern comes from similar people adopting, not from timing.

Method adoption models

Discrete-time logit of first adoption with year, method and lab effects and faculty-clustered errors. "Not through a joint paper": adoptions where no earlier adopter is a co-author on the adopting paper.

Do groups of three close into a joint paper?

Triples of faculty active in both periods who never shared a paper in 2016–2020: share who write one together in 2021–2025, by how many of the three pairs had co-authored and whether all those ties were repeated (log scale). Triples within one lab vs spanning labs.

Can the tie model grow the real network?

Each year adds as many links as really formed, drawn with the fitted model's probabilities on the simulated network. Values relative to the real network (1 = matches), mean of 40 simulations; each variant drops one ingredient and is refitted.

Did pandemic collaborations last?

Faculty pairs whose first joint paper (2020–22) was on SARS-CoV-2 / COVID-19 vs on other topics: share who wrote another paper together later, and another paper on a non-COVID topic. 95% intervals.

Citing before collaborating

Odds ratios from the tie-formation model with an extra term: had either faculty member cited the other's work (no shared author) before the year? 95% dyadic-robust intervals, log scale.

Every test, corrected together

      Spreading on the co-authorship network

      An idea, a technique or a rumour can spread between people who write together. We simulate the two classic epidemic models on the faculty network: SIS (susceptible → infected → susceptible again, like an idea that has to be kept alive) and SIR (infected people recover for good, like news that spreads once). Each simulation is repeated with many random seeds and compared with networks that keep everyone's number of co-authors but scramble who is linked to whom. Computed by step s23.

        What a flag means, and what it does not

        A flag marks a number that needs a closer look before it is compared: citations that come mostly from many-author papers, a profile that may merge two people, or an institution that cites the labs more than its size suggests. It is not a judgement of any person or institution and says nothing about misconduct. Consortium papers are legitimate science, heavy citation has ordinary causes (shared fields, former lab members, review articles), and OpenAlex itself makes identity errors. Individual citing authors are not named here.

        Papers with 100 or more authors

        Flagged faculty

        Flagged when 25% or more of the citations come from papers with 100+ authors, or 60% or more from papers with 10+ authors (faculty with at least 100 citations). "Without" columns recompute the citations and h-index leaving the 100+ author papers out.

        Lifetime citations: the "one lakh" check

        Lifetime OpenAlex citations of each faculty member (all their author profiles) against the citations of their 2016–2026 lab papers. A lifetime count of a lakh or more, or a large lifetime count with under 5% of it in the window, is flagged. In our data this usually means an OpenAlex profile that merges several people, or a long career before the window, so a flag is a prompt to check the profile, not a claim about the person.

        Do they still stand out once size is taken into account?

        Big institutions give more citations simply because they publish more. Each point divides an institution's citations to the lab(s) by its own output: articles and reviews of 2016–2026 in the labs' main fields (medicine, biochemistry and genetics, chemistry, immunology and microbiology, agricultural and biological sciences, neuroscience, pharmacology; about 88% of the labs' papers). Institutions with at least 30 citations and 300 such papers are compared. The dashed line is a robust z-score of 2 on the log of the rate (median and MAD of the compared group); the tick on each row is that group's median; flagged non-government institutions have a dark outline. Output counts come from OpenAlex.
